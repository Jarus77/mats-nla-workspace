Do NLAs read what the model can't say?

We test whether natural-language autoencoders (NLAs) and Oracle Lens read content in Qwen3.6-27B's activations that sits outside the model's verbal "workspace", or whether their success comes from the prompt text.

Prep + pilot complete Confirmatory sprint Oct 19 – Nov 1 Layer 42 · J-space rank 384 / 1536
Where the evidence points so far

On the one natural feature that lives outside the workspace and that the model actually uses, neither reader describes it, though both read workspace features at the same steering strength. Two narrow channels look like exceptions, and the sprint will test them.

The NLA does carry some content beyond the workspace (tense). On WorkspaceBench, the information in both readers' outputs is recoverable from the prompt alone.

The setup in three readers

J-Lens is a fixed linear map from an activation to the tokens the model is about to say. It defines the workspace: the top r directions the model can put into words. Oracle Lens is the same model with a small adapter, trained to describe an injected activation. The NLA is a separate fine-tuned verbalizer, paired with a reconstructor, that reads only layer 42.

The hard part is telling reading from re-solving. A reader that reconstructs the prompt and answers it looks just like one that reads the hidden computation, so the key experiments steer the activation while keeping the text fixed.

What we found

  • ReplicatedThe tools work as published. NLA reconstruction 0.761 vs 0.766 published; layer convention verified at cosine 0.9999; all 12/12 WorkspaceBench numbers inside our 95% intervals.
  • CalibratedThe standard "outside the workspace" test can't tell features apart. Every feature keeps at least 98% of its probe accuracy after removing the workspace directions, topic and language included. With a test calibrated against random subspaces, the content outside the workspace on Qwen looks like positional bookkeeping: line length, indentation, sentence position.
  • CalibratedLine length ("col") is a clean test case. It is outside the workspace under two different lenses, the model can't report it, and it keeps 79% of its effect on behaviour after the workspace is removed, against 12–23% for every other feature we tested.
  • MeasuredOracle Lens's benchmark lead is a scoring choice. Counting an item if any of 11 layers gets it adds 0.08–0.22. At the same layer, the NLA ties or beats it.
  • MeasuredWorkspaceBench success is recoverable from the prompt. A text model given the prompt names the target more often than one given the readers' outputs, and adding the outputs to the prompt doesn't help. The benchmark's stored prompt-only floor also reads about 0.14 too low.
  • PilotReaders track workspace features but don't describe col. At equal steering strength, both follow language and tense while col stays at chance. Oracle Lens's next-token channel and one NLA phrasing do respond to col; both are pre-registered for confirmation.
  • PilotOracle Lens's line-break channel moves one-for-one with the model's own newline decision. Its shift per unit of steering matches the model's (ratio 1.06), and both keep the same share after the workspace is removed (0.79 vs 0.75). The same channel also tracks tense where the model's next-token behaviour doesn't move, so it is more than the model's decision re-emitted. Either way, it is not a readout anyone would find without already knowing what to measure.
  • PilotThe NLA invents a consistent story. When we plant a different country in the activation, it rewrites the prompt's clue to fit: "Eiffel Tower" became "Volvo… Sweden" in 12/12 cases. Its "prompt reconstruction" comes from the latent, not the text.

Limits to keep in mind

  • Only one natural feature (col) passed every gate, so generalisation is open.
  • Pilot results are small-sample and were found after many looks; they need the sprint to stand.
  • Whether Oracle Lens reports col or re-instates it for its own model can't be settled by the effect-ratio test, because the two contexts differ. We report the measurements without a verdict.
  • The strong findings today are about the benchmark and the test: WorkspaceBench can't separate reading from re-solving, and the standard workspace test can't tell features apart. Whether the readers read non-workspace content is the sprint's question.
  • One model (Qwen3.6-27B), one layer (42), two J-Lens fits.
